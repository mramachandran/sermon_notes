Herod the Great

The brilliant, ruthless king who rebuilt the temple and tried to kill the newborn King of the Jews.

Herod the Great ruled Judaea for Rome for more than thirty years. A gifted builder and shrewd politician, he rebuilt the temple on a grand scale. He was also deeply suspicious and cruel. The Jewish historian Josephus records that he executed one of his wives and three of his own sons when he feared plots against his throne. So when wise men arrived asking for the newborn "King of the Jews," Herod was troubled. He pretended he wanted to worship the child, then ordered the killing of Bethlehem's baby boys. Soon afterward he died. Herod's story shows the fear of a man clinging to power, set against the quiet arrival of the true King.

The life of Herod the Great

Dates are approximate (BC). Most scholars date his death to 4 BC. A minority prefer 1 BC.

  1. 40 BCNamed king

    Rome's choice

    With help from Mark Antony and Octavian, the Roman Senate declares Herod king of Judaea. He has to fight for three years to take his kingdom.

    Josephus, Antiquities 14
  2. 37 BCJerusalem taken

    Reign begins

    With Roman troops, Herod captures Jerusalem and becomes king in fact. He marries Mariamne, from the old Jewish royal family, to strengthen his claim.

    Josephus, Antiquities 14
  3. c. 20/19 BC18th year

    Temple rebuilding begins

    Herod starts a vast rebuilding of the temple and its courts. Work continues long after his death; in Jesus' day it had been "forty and six years" in building.

    John 2:20; Josephus, Antiquities 15
  4. c. 6–4 BCWise men arrive

    Troubled

    Magi ask for the newborn King of the Jews. Herod gathers the chief priests and scribes, meets the wise men secretly, and sends them to Bethlehem.

    Matthew 2:1–8
  5. c. 6–4 BCBethlehem

    The killing of the boys

    Seeing that the wise men have mocked him by not returning, Herod in great anger orders every boy two years old and under in and around Bethlehem killed.

    Matthew 2:16–18
  6. c. 4 BCAt Jericho

    Death

    Herod dies, and an angel tells Joseph in Egypt that those who sought the child's life are dead. His kingdom is divided among his sons.

    Matthew 2:19–22; Josephus, Antiquities 17

Key activities

Only Matthew records the killing of the infants. Bethlehem was a small town, so many scholars think the number of boys was small. Josephus does not mention it, though it fits the cruelty he describes.

The world around them

Rome had controlled Judaea since 63 BC. It preferred to rule through local kings who kept order and paid tribute. Herod was an Idumean, from the people descended from Esau whom the Hasmonean rulers had forced to accept Judaism. Many Jews never fully accepted him. He kept power through spies, fortresses and executions. After his death, Augustus divided the kingdom: Archelaus received Judaea, Antipas received Galilee and Perea, and Philip received lands to the north-east (Luke 3:1).

Words and places

  • Idumea. Greek name for Edom, south of Judaea, home of Herod's family (Mark 3:8).
  • Chief priests and scribes. The leading priests and the experts in the Law of Moses (Matthew 2:4).
  • Ramah. A town in Benjamin, north of Jerusalem, where Jeremiah pictures Rachel weeping (Jeremiah 31:15; Matthew 2:18).
  • Archelaus. Herod's son who ruled Judaea after him (Matthew 2:22).
