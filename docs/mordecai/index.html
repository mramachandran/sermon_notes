Mordecai

The Jew at the king's gate who raised Esther, would not bow to Haman, and rose to be second only to the king.

Mordecai was a Jewish official in the Persian royal city of Shushan who raised his orphaned cousin Esther as his own daughter. From the king's gate he uncovered a plot to kill the king. When he refused to bow to Haman, Haman plotted to destroy all the Jews. Mordecai put on sackcloth and urged Esther to act "for such a time as this." In the end the king gave him Haman's place, and he wrote the decree that saved his people. He and Esther set up the feast of Purim.

The life of Mordecai

Dates approximate (BC). Esther 2:6 says someone "carried away" with Jeconiah in 597 BC: Mordecai himself or his great-grandfather Kish. If Mordecai, he would have been very old by Xerxes' reign.

  1. Before Esther became queen

    Adopts his cousin

    Mordecai brings up the orphaned Esther as his daughter and daily checks on her at the palace.

    Esther 2:5–11
  2. c. 479–474 BCAt the king's gate

    Saves the king's life

    He uncovers a plot by two doorkeepers. It is recorded, but he receives no reward.

    Esther 2:19–23
  3. c. 474 BC12th year

    Refuses to bow

    The king promotes Haman and commands everyone at the gate to bow to him. Mordecai will not. When Haman learns that Mordecai is a Jew, he plots to destroy all the Jews.

    Esther 3:1–6
  4. c. 474 BCThe same month

    Sackcloth and a challenge

    Mordecai tears his clothes and cries out in the city. He sends Esther a copy of the decree and urges her to plead with the king: "who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

    Esther 4:1–17
  5. The night before the second banquet

    Honoured by his enemy

    That night the sleepless king hears the record of Mordecai's loyalty and learns he was never rewarded. Haman, who came to ask for Mordecai's death, must lead him through the city on the king's horse.

    Esther 6:1–12
  6. c. 474–473 BCGiven Haman's place

    The new decree and Purim

    The king gives Mordecai the signet ring he took from Haman. Mordecai writes a decree letting the Jews defend themselves and goes out in royal robes. Afterwards he sends letters setting up the yearly feast of Purim.

    Esther 8:1–2, 9–17; 9:20–23

Key activities

Why would Mordecai not bow? The text says only that "he had told them that he was a Jew" (Esther 3:4). Some think bowing to Haman seemed too close to worship. Others point to an old feud: Haman was an Agagite, perhaps descended from Agag, king of Amalek, while Mordecai came from the family of Kish, like King Saul (1 Samuel 15).

The world around them

Shushan, also called Susa, was one of the royal cities of the Persian kings, in what is now south-west Iran. In the ancient Near East the city gate was where officials sat, business was done and cases were judged. So when Esther says Mordecai "sat in the king's gate," it probably means he held some royal post. Persia was famous for its fast royal messengers, who carried the king's letters across the empire. Bowing low before a superior was an ordinary Persian custom, which makes Mordecai's refusal stand out all the more.

Words and places

  • The king's gate. The entrance to the palace complex, where royal officials sat (Esther 2:19, 21).
  • Sackcloth. Rough cloth worn as a sign of grief or repentance (Esther 4:1).
  • The king's ring. A signet ring used to seal royal decrees. Whoever held it spoke with the king's authority (Esther 3:10; 8:2, 8).
  • Purim. From pur, "the lot." Haman cast lots to pick the day to destroy the Jews (Esther 3:7; 9:24–26).
