Philemon and Onesimus

A master and his slave, both brought to Christ through Paul, and a letter asking one to welcome the other.

Paul's shortest letter is a personal appeal about two men. Philemon was a believer, probably in Colossae, who hosted a church in his home. Onesimus was his slave, who had left him and possibly wronged him. Somehow Onesimus came to Paul in prison, became a believer, and was a real help to Paul. Paul sent him back asking Philemon to receive him "not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved," and offered to pay whatever he owed. We are not told how Philemon responded. The gospel reaches into the most unequal relationships and calls for reconciliation.

The life of Philemon and Onesimus

Dates are approximate (AD). Most place the letter during Paul's imprisonment in Rome, c. AD 60–62. Some prefer an earlier imprisonment at Ephesus or the one in Caesarea.

  1. EarlierProbably Colossae

    Philemon believes

    Philemon comes to faith, apparently through Paul, who reminds him that he owes Paul "even thine own self." A church meets in his house.

    Philemon 1–2, 19
  2. Before the letter

    Onesimus leaves

    Onesimus, Philemon's slave, is parted from his master. Paul hints that he may have wronged Philemon or owed him something.

    Philemon 15–16, 18
  3. c. AD 60–62With Paul in prison

    Onesimus believes

    Onesimus comes to Paul and becomes a believer. Paul calls him "my son Onesimus, whom I have begotten in my bonds." He becomes dear and useful to Paul.

    Philemon 10–13
  4. c. AD 60–62Sent home

    Carrying a letter

    Paul sends Onesimus back with Tychicus and calls him "a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you."

    Colossians 4:7–9; Philemon 12
  5. On arrival

    Philemon's choice

    Philemon must decide how to receive him. Paul is confident he will do "more than I say," and asks him to prepare a room for Paul's hoped-for visit.

    Philemon 17–22

Key activities

Christians have read this letter in different ways. Paul does not directly command Philemon to free Onesimus, though some read "thou wilt also do more than I say" (verse 21) as a hint. Both sides of later debates over slavery appealed to it.

The world around them

Slavery was part of everyday life in the Roman world, and slaves made up a large share of the population in many cities, though estimates vary widely. Roman slavery was not based on race, and many slaves were eventually freed. Yet slaves were legally property and could be harshly punished, and a runaway faced severe penalties. Some scholars think Onesimus was not a runaway at all. They suggest he went to Paul, a friend of his master, to ask him to plead his case, a practice known from Roman sources.

Words and places

  • Servant. In the KJV of Philemon, the Greek word for a slave (Philemon 16).
  • Bowels. In KJV English, the deep feelings of the heart. Paul calls Onesimus "mine own bowels" (Philemon 12).
  • Colossae. A small city in the Lycus valley of Phrygia, near Laodicea (Colossians 1:2; 4:13).
  • Tychicus. Paul's messenger, who carried the letter to Colossae with Onesimus (Colossians 4:7–9).
